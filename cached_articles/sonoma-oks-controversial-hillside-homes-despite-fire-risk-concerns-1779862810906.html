Sonoma OKs controversial Brazil Hillside homes despite fire risk concerns

  • Email a link to a friend (Opens in new window) Email
By Emma Molloy | emma.molloy@sonomanews.com | Sonoma Index-Tribune
PUBLISHED: May 22, 2026 at 12:56 AM PDT | UPDATED: May 26, 2026 at 12:13 PM PDT
Getting your Trinity Audio player ready...

After more than a decade of legal wrangling, design revisions and neighborhood anxiety, the Sonoma City Council on Wednesday, May 20, unanimously approved three large hillside homes above Brazil Street, concluding a significant, contentious land-use saga in the city.

Council’s 5-0 approval of the plan for a new home behind 149 Fourth St. E. and two homes on Lots 227 and 228 Brazil Street took place during a regular meeting that included a land-use public hearing which focused largely on fire safety, tree removal, drainage, and the limits of local control in a very high fire severity zone.

The road to approval for the Brazil Hillside Projects — a trio of single-family homes on adjacent slopes of Schocken Hill — has been marked by a decade of community opposition and complex legal maneuvers. The initial proposal was for development of two 5,000-square-foot homes at 227 and 228 Brazil St., and an 11,000-square-foot main residence at 149 Fourth St. East.

First entering the city’s orbit in 2015 then 2016 when the initial applications were submitted, the project received approval from the Sonoma Planning Commission the following year. However, neighborhood groups quickly appealed, arguing the large-scale designs violated the city’s Hillside Ordinance, which was intended to protect Sonoma’s scenic ridgelines from urban sprawl.

In May 2018, the City Council sided with residents in a 3-2 vote, denying the project. This sparked a lawsuit from the developer, accusing the city of violating the Housing Accountability Act, which limits local oversight on residential projects.

The deadlock finally broke in early 2021 through a legal settlement that determined the owners are entitled to develop the land, as long as the projects meet strict new rules under the city’s standards and hillside ordinances. Under the agreement, the property owners were required to submit significantly scaled-down designs, including a “stepped” architecture to follow the hill’s natural contours and a total ban on second stories.

The settlement also mandated that the new applications bypass the Planning Commission entirely, moving directly to the City Council for the final public hearing, which took place May 20.

Since the initial proposals were submitted, the applicant has reduced the total project footprint by more than 12,000 square feet, at the request of the city.

Community Development Director Jennifer Gates presented the updated proposals to council members, along with the project engineer Chad Moll and project architect Kenneth Holder with Holder Parlette Architecture of Kentfield, California.

Moll noted the project’s grueling timeline. “I started working on this project in 2015. So, more than 10 years ago,” he noted, describing the years spent reworking designs with city staff to ensure compliance.

The revised plans call for three homes on parcels ranging from 2 acres to 2.9 acres and foundation sizes are capped at 5,000 square feet for the Brazil Street lots and 11,000 square feet for the Fourth Street East property.

Holder said, “The overall intent was to keep this building mapped as closely to the site as possible, following the cues from the natural topography … the house will be largely screened by the existing vegetation.”

The potential for increased water runoff that could impact residents living below the site remained a primary concern.

“I look at the west side of Fourth Street East, the drainage system that is owned by the city, and the condition is so bad. I’ve checked the neighborhood during flooding, they’ve always had difficulties. The developers should contact the city and see how we can improve storm and water drainage,” Council member Jack Ding said.

While Public Works Director Mike Berger noted that the projects would include bio-retention planters and interim soil logs to manage runoff, Council member Patricia Farrar-Rivas said those would not “guarantee that in extreme weather events there isn’t water coming down the hill,” and that “it goes a long way to make it better than it is right now, but still there’s going to be instances where there will be water coming down.”

Much of the night’s discussion about the Brazil Hillside plans was calm and even, though tensions did elevate somewhat when talk moved to fire risk mitigation and the state’s mandate to clear vegetation and debris from property in order to create defensible spaces, and Sonoma’s local tree-protection ordinance, which states that any trees removed have to be replaced.

Sonoma Mayor Ron Wellander called the discussion “counterintuitive,”  while the attorney for the projects’ developer asked that any trees removed during construction, specifically to comply with state fire laws, be exempt from the city’s one-for-one replacement requirement.

Sonoma Valley Fire District Deputy Chief/Fire Marshal Trevor Smith, in an attempt to ease tensions, told council members not to be concerned about the loss of any mature trees due to this development.

“Are trees an issue? No. Mature trees typically don’t represent a hazard,” Smith said. He added that a developed, well-maintained site could actually slow fire progression by creating “reduced fuel zones.”

The discussion also touched on the lack of affordable housing in the proposal, a point raised by Vice Mayor Sandra Lowe.

While the developers will contribute $50,000 to the city’s Affordable Housing Trust Fund per the settlement, Lowe noted, “It just kind of breaks my heart a little bit that when we do have projects that come forward that do affordable housing components, I’d like them to be received as politely and earnestly as we are potentially approving this one.”

The city’s consultant, AECOM, concluded the three hillside homes qualified for a class 3 categorical exemption under the California Environmental Equality Act (CEQA), which covers small-scale new construction such as up to three single-family residences. Relying on that finding, the council approved the project without requiring a full environmental impact report.

At the end of the hearing, besides unanimously approving the three projects, council members granted six years for completion and OK’d the developer’s request about tree clearance.

Longtime property owner Bill Jasper, who initiated the project 12 years ago, thanked council members for their approval.

“We think it’s been a very collaborative process, where we gave, you gave, and we came to a great decision,” he said. “I just want to express our appreciation to what seems like a different attitude than we had for so many years.”
